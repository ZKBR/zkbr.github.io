What is a zero-knowledge proof?

Zero-knowledge proofs let one computer convince another that a computation is correct, without redoing it, and sometimes without revealing the data behind it. This page explains the idea in three steps. The last one is a zero-knowledge proof you can play yourself.

Based on the talk Zero knowledge, or how to outsource a computation verifiably by Jeroen van de Graaf (DCC/UFMG).

2 Private and succinct are different things

Two properties are often mixed up. A proof is succinct if it is short and quick to check. It is zero-knowledge if the verifier learns nothing beyond the fact that the claim is true. A proof can have either property without the other. Click an example to place it.

Choose an example.

Beware of the name. The original meaning of zero knowledge is privacy. Today the label “zk” is often used for proofs that are only succinct, as in many “zkVMs” and “zk-rollups”. Whether a given system really hides its inputs has to be checked case by case.

3 Play a zero-knowledge proof

A Hamiltonian circuit is a round trip through a graph that visits every vertex exactly once and returns to the start. Finding one in a large graph is hard. The prover claims to know a circuit in the graph below and will convince you without showing it. You are the verifier. This is Blum’s protocol (1986).

This is a toy example: with 6 vertices there are only 60 possible round trips, and a computer tries them all instantly. But their number, (n−1)!/2, grows faster than exponentially: for 100 vertices it is about 10155. The problem is NP-complete, and no known algorithm avoids exponential time. With a few hundred vertices, finding the circuit is out of reach, yet the proof works exactly the same way.

How it works
  1. Commit. The prover renames the vertices at random, giving a scrambled copy of the graph, and seals each entry of its adjacency matrix in a box. Here a box is a SHA-256 hash of the bit with a random nonce: it shows nothing about the bit, and the prover cannot change the bit later.
  2. Challenge. You flip a coin, or choose.
  3. Respond. On 0, the prover opens all boxes and the renaming, and you check that the boxes hold a faithful copy of the public graph. On 1, the prover opens only the boxes of a circuit in the scrambled copy, and you check they form a round trip through all six vertices.

Why it convinces. A prover who does not know a circuit can prepare for only one of the two challenges, so it is caught with probability ½ in each round. After 20 rounds, the chance it fooled you is below one in a million.

Why you learn nothing. On 0 you see a random renaming of a graph you already knew. On 1 you see a round trip through six unnamed points. You could have produced either view yourself, without the prover. That is the formal definition of zero knowledge (Goldwasser, Micali and Rackoff, 1985): everything the verifier sees can be simulated without the secret.

Why it is not succinct. Each round sends a sealed box per matrix entry, and you need many rounds. For a graph with a thousand vertices, that is millions of boxes. SNARKs get the same kind of guarantee with a proof of a few hundred bytes.

4 Where this is used

Private payments

Prove a transaction is valid without revealing sender, receiver or amount, as in Zcash.

Credentials

Prove you are over 18, or a citizen, without showing the document itself.

Machine learning (zkML)

Prove that a model produced a given output, without revealing the model or the data.

Rollups

Run thousands of transactions off the blockchain and post one short proof that the new state is correct. This uses succinctness more than privacy.

The ZKBR group studies how these systems work, how fast they are and whether they really hide what they claim. See the papers we follow and the team.
