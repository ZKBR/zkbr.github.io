What is post-quantum cryptography?

A large quantum computer would break the public-key cryptography that protects almost all internet traffic today. Post-quantum cryptography replaces it with problems that quantum computers are not known to solve. This page shows what would break, and lets you try one of the new problems yourself.

Based on the talk Post-quantum cryptography, or how a working quantum computer will destroy four decades of cryptographic research by Jeroen van de Graaf (DCC Week, UFMG, 2026).

1 What a quantum computer would break

Shor’s algorithm (1994): broken

Factoring and discrete logarithms become easy. That breaks RSA, Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography: the key exchange behind HTTPS, the signatures on certificates, and the keys of Bitcoin and Ethereum wallets.

Fix: replace them with different problems.

Grover’s algorithm (1996): weakened

Exhaustive search over 2N keys takes about 2N/2 steps. That weakens AES and hash functions such as SHA-256, but does not break them.

Fix: double the key size, for example AES-256 instead of AES-128.

No quantum computer today comes close: the largest number factored with Shor’s algorithm proper is 21, and breaking RSA would need thousands of error-corrected logical qubits, each made of many noisy physical ones. Still, the migration has started. NIST plans to retire RSA and elliptic curves by 2035, and anything encrypted today can be recorded now and decrypted once such a computer exists: harvest now, decrypt later.

2 Try it: learning with errors

Most of the new standards rest on lattices, and the central problem is learning with errors (LWE). It is plain linear algebra modulo a number q, with one twist: every equation is off by a small error. Below are four equations in two unknowns, modulo q = 97. Your goal is to find the secret x.

Attack 1: Gaussian elimination

Solve the first two equations exactly, then check the result against the other two.

Attack 2: try every x

Test all 97 × 97 = 9,409 candidates: x is a solution if every equation is off by at most 1.

Now make it bigger

Elimination fails as soon as there is an error, so an attacker is left with searching. With n unknowns there are qn candidates.

Why this is the right kind of hard

Without the error, the equations Ax = b are solved in an instant by Gaussian elimination, whatever their size. With the error, elimination combines the equations and the small errors add up into large ones, so the answer it gives is wrong. That is what you saw in attack 1.

Searching works for two unknowns, but the number of candidates grows exponentially with n. The best known attacks are cleverer than searching: they use lattice reduction. Still, they take exponential time too, on classical and on quantum computers. No quantum algorithm like Shor’s is known for LWE.

Real parameters. ML-KEM-512, the smallest version of the new NIST key-exchange standard, works with 512 unknowns modulo q = 3,329, with a structured matrix to keep keys small. Its security is estimated to be comparable to AES-128.

From problem to encryption. The public key is the pair (A, b). To encrypt a bit, the sender adds up a random selection of the equations and hides the bit in the result. Only someone who knows x can remove the noise and read the bit (Regev, 2005).

Two other families were broken during the selection process: Rainbow (multivariate polynomials) and SIKE (isogenies). Having standards based on different problems is a hedge in case one of them falls. The migration is already under way: Chrome and Cloudflare use a hybrid of X25519 and ML-KEM, and Signal and iMessage have added post-quantum key exchange.

The ZKBR group follows this field because zero-knowledge proof systems are affected too: many rest on elliptic curves. See the slides of the talk for more, including what the migration means for Bitcoin and Ethereum.
